### DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer inference framework: an offline release covering only one model and two types of chips

- What happened: On 10-20 at 17:30, DeepSeek open-sourced the DeepInfer inference framework under the Apache-2.0 license. The company says it is the inference framework supporting DeepSeek's large-scale production serving, and that what was released this time is the offline inference version, bundled with the core capabilities required for large-scale serving. The deepseek-recipe codec component was open-sourced at the same time (according to the official post and Chinese-language retellings).
- Why it matters: This is putting one layer of its own production stack out in public, rather than shipping a demo repository. But the scope is drawn very clearly: one model, two types of chips, offline inference; the KV cache, Prefill/Decode disaggregation, and cross-replica load balancing needed to actually run inference as a service were not released. For teams hoping to replicate DeepSeek's serving setup, this code is a starting point, not an answer.
- Original post and engagement: [original post](https://x.com/deepseek_ai/status/2112476496283775135) (10-20 17:30, 3.67 million views, 32,000 likes, 3,723 reposts, 1,229 replies, 1,873 quotes, 12,000 bookmarks).
- Verification: Cross-checked against the [GitHub repository README](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepInfer), which confirms Apache-2.0; the README explicitly states that the framework "powers DeepSeek's large-scale production serving" and that this open-source release provides offline inference; the only supported model is DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash; the only supported hardware listed is NVIDIA SM100 and Huawei Ascend 950; the KV cache system, Prefill/Decode disaggregation, and cross-replica load balancing are listed as "required for a complete inference service but not included in this release."
- Judgment: What was opened is part of a production stack, not a complete service. The three components that actually determine whether it can be deployed are not in the repository, and single-model support means that in the near term it is more of a reference implementation for V4.1-Flash; the inclusion of Ascend 950 is the most noteworthy line in this README, but between "supported" and "able to serve reliably" there is still a lack of practical data.

- Judgment: This is a concession at the infrastructure level, and the beneficiaries are coding and ops Agents that need persistent context. ### humans& releases its first model, Ampersand

- What happened: On 10-21 at 06:37, humans& released its first model, Ampersand, saying that in simulations lasting several months, users collaborating with Ampersand achieved higher completion rates than with frontier models such as Fable, and that the API is open to everyone.
- Why it matters: The company's hypothesis is that "models should be trained for humans and models to complete tasks together," and its evaluation framing is switched to collaboration value rather than single-turn accuracy, with interaction cost counted as a penalty. This is a direct challenge to the current benchmark system: if multiple rounds of back-and-forth themselves consume the user, then the strongest single-turn model is not necessarily the most useful.
- Original post and engagement: [original post](https://x.com/humansand/status/2112674550907552137) (10-21 06:37, 27,000 views, 332 likes).
- Verification: Cross-checked against the [official blog](https://ampersand.humansand.ai/blog/) (published 10-20), which confirms that Ampersand v0.1 is offered through the Agents API; it is fine-tuned on GLM 5.3 (744B); the evaluation method simulates a user population over several months of simulated time; the company itself acknowledges that interaction cost is higher than other leading models. The claim of being "better than Fable" is the vendor's self-assessment.
- Judgment: The base model is a fine-tuned GLM 5.3, which shows that this is innovation at the product and training-objective layers, not a new model trained from scratch. The company's acknowledgment of higher interaction cost amounts to pointing out its own weakness: the collaboration benefit must be large enough to cover the cost of multiple rounds of back-and-forth for the case to hold. This kind of self-assessment is hard for third parties to reproduce in the short term; watch real users' task completion rates first.
